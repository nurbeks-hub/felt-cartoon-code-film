The ask

Nurbek

Can we do something bright now? Cartoons, for kids. You went too far into cinema. Let's try other directions. What can you offer? You understand what you're capable of now.

Three styles were proposed: clay, felt and paper, and bright 3D. The answer was short.

Nurbek

Let's do all three.

Each style got its own chat. The lead chat wrote this brief for the felt one (shortened, original wording):

Lead brief to the felt chat

Make a 20-second bright children's cartoon in a world of felt and paper […]: fuzz on felt, cardboard sets with visible layers and edges, threads, buttons, stitches, painted paper — entirely in our own code.

A story with a hook in the first second and a funny or touching twist; ideally the twist plays on the handmade material (a thread unravels, a paper sun is lifted by a hand-stick).

Generate targets: turnarounds of the heroes and 6–8 styleframes. They are a target for comparison; they never go into the frame. Then models in code, side by side with the refs; an animatic under music; the final render; sound.

Rendering only on remote GPUs. Show the styleframes and the animatic before the final render.

A story that runs on the material

A tiny felt chick crows at dawn, but the paper sun will not rise. It climbs the cardboard hill and finds the sun asleep on the far end of the moon's yarn, hung over a wooden spool. Hanging on the yarn with all its weight, it hauls the sun up. The twist: the moment it lets go to celebrate, the sun drops and night falls again. So it ties the yarn to a fat sleeping sheep, who now holds the day up while snoring.

The whole film was timed to a bar map of the music before anything was modelled: the sunrise sits on the drop at 11.1 s, and the music stops for the gag between 13.4 and 15.7 s. Tap a time to jump the film there.

4

Teaching our renderer felt and paper

The renderer is our own path tracer: it traces how light bounces through the scene, on the GPU through WebGPU, inside headless Chrome. The integrator, acceleration structure, materials, camera, denoiser and tone mapping are our own code. It ran on rented Runpod GPUs and a remote RTX 5090, never on the Mac.

The felt chat copied the engine and taught its copy two new materials:

  • Felt fuzz. A sheen layer, the soft glow that velvet and wool show at grazing angles, so the felt lights up along its contours in backlight.
  • Paper that lets light through. Diffuse transmission for thin paper, so the moon, the stars and the sun glow from behind.
  • Plus bump strength on materials, for the fibre surface.

The textures are generated in code as well: felt, paper and corrugated cardboard, written with numpy. Geometry comes from a small kit: felt pillows and paper cut-outs grown from 2-D shapes, tubes for yarn, wire and stitches, and thousands of surface fibres for the fuzz. The chick alone carries about 9,000 fibres and about 300,000 triangles; a full scene is about 2.4 million.

Animatic and the lead's notes

The whole film was first rendered small and noisy (640 × 480, 40 samples per pixel) with the music, synthesised sound effects and a title, to check story and timing before the expensive render.

The felt chat listed its own fixes: the third shot repeated the angle of the first; the sleeping sun had to be pushed to the centre of frame; the chick was too small in the sunrise and the gag; it walked through the sheep in the last shot; the night was murky.

Lead, on the animatic

The story and timing read, and your list of composition fixes is right — do all of it. But the main gap with the styleframes is the look. The felt must be visible: a fuzz halo along the contour glowing in backlight, seams with stitches, hills as stacked felt layers with the corrugated cardboard edge visible in the cut. The night is flat: it needs a deep-blue night with a warm glow from the paper moon and stars. The sun and moon are tiny in the wide shots: bring the mechanics closer so they read.

The composition fixes went into this film. The bigger look notes arrived after the final render had started, so they are being worked into a separate look pass; the film on this page is that first finished cut with the sound and title of v1.1.

8

The render on rented GPUs

Nothing heavy ran on the Mac. The chat brought up its own Runpod pods with RTX 4090 cards (at $0.89 an hour each), checked that WebGPU saw the NVIDIA card, and split the frames between them.

  • The animatic: 480 frames across three pods.
  • The final: 1440 × 1080, 192 samples per pixel, on four pods. About 20 seconds of wall time per frame on one 4090, mostly spent packing and uploading the scene's 2.4 million triangles.
  • After review, frames 0–11 and 158–479 were rendered again on three pods.
  • Each pod was deleted as soon as its part was done, and every deletion was checked.
9

Review, frame by frame

Before anything reaches Nurbek, a machine pass measures every frame and every audio sample, flags jumps, holds and pops, and gives a reviewer one frame per shot to look at. Then a fresh agent with no context watches the film cold.

The fresh eyes gave the first cut 4 out of 10.

What it found, and what was fixed:

  • A cut between two shots from the same angle looked like a mistake. It became one continuous shot with a comic push-in.
  • The gag did not read: the chick was tiny and the release was invisible. Now the release, the clap and the yarn zipping up play in a medium shot, and the chick turns and stares into the lens.
  • The yarn looked glued to the chick's crest. Now it hangs in profile, holding the yarn in its beak.
  • The moon passed through the chick. It now lands in front of the moon, and the moon and sun get motion blur.
  • The yarn crossed the sheep's face. The loop moved round the body.
  • The opening was static. The nest now trembles before the spring pops.

The second machine pass showed only intended or minor flags. Remaining weak spots are written down: the chick is small in the wide shot at 2–4 s, and the sheep's fleece looks greyish.
